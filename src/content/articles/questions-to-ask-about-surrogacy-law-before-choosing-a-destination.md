Almost every "where is surrogacy legal" page on the internet is written by someone who sells programs in the countries it recommends. That is not a conspiracy. It is simply that the parties with the most traffic are the parties with a commercial reason to rank destinations, and ranking is not something anyone can do honestly without data.

So the useful move is to stop looking for a ranking and build a comparison instead. Six dimensions do most of the work, and each can be answered by a document rather than by an opinion.

## The six dimensions

### 1. Enforceability of the arrangement

Is the agreement enforceable in that jurisdiction, and what do the parties rely on if it is not? This is not the same as whether the arrangement is permitted. A place can allow an arrangement and still give neither side a contract to point at.

### 2. Parentage at birth, and the route from there

Who is the legal mother and legal father when the child is born, what court process changes that, who can apply, and how long it takes. A destination where you become the legal parent at birth and a destination where you become one eight months later through a court are different places to plan a family in, even when the treatment is identical.

### 3. Eligibility for you specifically

Genetic connection requirements, marital or relationship status, age, citizenship and residency. This is the dimension where a destination most often looks open and turns out not to be, and it is the one that depends on facts about you that no published page has.

### 4. What may lawfully be paid

Reimbursement only, expenses with court approval, negotiated compensation, or prohibition. This decides both your budget and your legal exposure, and it is the dimension most often flattened into a single word like "commercial" or "altruistic" on a comparison table.

### 5. Presence and practical constraints

How many trips, how long you must be in-country, whether you can be at the birth, who may be on the birth certificate, and what happens if a flight is cancelled in the third trimester.

### 6. Getting the child home

Registration, the document that lets the child travel, and what your own country requires to recognise the parentage. This dimension is not part of the destination's law, and treating it as part of the destination decision is how people end up with a child they cannot bring home.

## The source standard to insist on

For each of those six, ask what kind of document the answer rests on. A rough hierarchy, from weakest to strongest:

1. **Program or agency marketing pages.** Written to convert, and often the actual origin of the figure another site cites.
2. **Law firm practice pages.** More precise about the law, still selling something, and capable of getting a statute number wrong.
3. **Professional body surveys and bar association guidance.** Written for practitioners, usually careful about scope, and still a summary rather than the law.
4. **Court decisions and enacted legislation or regulations.** What the system actually says. Readable, dated, and attributable.
5. **A lawyer admitted in that jurisdiction, on your facts.** The only source that can answer dimensions three and six for you.

When you read a destination claim, ask which level it comes from. If a page cannot say, it is repeating something. The [destination pages](/destinations) put the source and the date it was opened next to each claim for exactly this reason, and mark which claims rest on a law firm's or an academic's account rather than on the statute or the decision text itself, instead of smoothing them into a description of the law.

## Four traps that make most country comparisons worthless

**"Legal" treated as one state.** Legality is four questions, not one. A country can permit an arrangement, refuse to enforce it, require court approval for expenses, and leave parentage unresolved. The [legal framework](/guides/surrogacy-legal-considerations) separates these.

**Comparing one country's best case to another's average.** Programs advertise their smoothest path. Every destination has an ordinary case and a difficult one, and the difference between them is usually procedural rather than advertised.

**Ignoring what changed.** Surrogacy law moves. Reform proposals exist that have not been enacted, court decisions get reinterpreted, and administrative practice shifts without the statute changing. A comparison table with no dates on it is a snapshot of nobody knows when.

**Letting the fee decide.** Cost is the easiest dimension to compare and often the least consequential. A cheaper destination whose parentage route you cannot use, or whose documents your home country will not accept, is not cheaper.

## A working method

1. Write down your own constraints first: genetic connection available, relationship status, citizenship, time you can spend in-country, and the maximum you will commit before reassessing.
2. Shortlist on dimensions 1 to 4, using primary or professional sources, and note the date of each.
3. Put dimension 5 to clinics and agencies as a factual question with a written answer.
4. Take dimension 6, and anything unresolved from 3, to a lawyer in the destination **and** a lawyer where the child will live.
5. Compare the surviving options on documents, not on scores.

If you end up with two viable destinations and two quotes, the comparison in the [quote tool](/tools/agency-quote-comparison) is the same exercise on the financial side: same rows, same requirement to name a payee, and no total that quietly assumes nothing is missing.

This page is a method, not advice about any country or program. Nothing here determines whether a destination is available to you, and that question needs a qualified lawyer in the relevant jurisdiction.
