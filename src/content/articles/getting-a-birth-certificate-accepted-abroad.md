A child born through a surrogacy arrangement usually needs three separate documents to exist in two legal systems at once: a birth certificate from the place of birth, a proof of parentage that the receiving country accepts, and an identity document that lets the child cross a border. None of those is produced by the clinic or the agency, and the delay between a birth and a usable set is measured in weeks rather than days.

This is the part of the process that a package price never describes, because nobody in the arrangement is the one who decides it. The authority that will read your documents is not a party to your contract.

## What an apostille attaches to

The [Hague Convention of 5 October 1961](https://www.hcch.net/en/instruments/conventions/status-table/?cid=41) is the instrument that removed the old requirement to have a foreign public document legalised through consular channels, and replaced it with a single certificate attached to the document.

- It applies to public documents, which is the category a civil registration entry belongs to, not to the private paperwork of an arrangement.
- It is issued by a competent authority in the country where the document was issued, and only that country's authority can attach one.
- What the certificate says is about the document's origin. The [United Kingdom's own guidance](https://www.gov.uk/get-document-legalised) describes the office checking whether the signatures, stamps or seals on a document match its records, which is a statement about who signed it rather than a ruling on whether the facts inside it are true.
- It does not translate anything, and it does not make a document preferable to another document. A receiving authority can still require its own form of application, its own evidence, or its own decision.

That last bullet is where most of the surprise sits. An apostille removes a step, not the requirement to satisfy the authority at the other end.

## Who issues one, in the places this site covers

The Convention is in force in each of the five jurisdictions with a record here, but the issuing office differs, and the [Convention's status table](https://www.hcch.net/en/instruments/conventions/status-table/?cid=41) gives the dates rather than the addresses.

1. **United States.** A birth certificate is a state document, so it is apostilled by the competent authority designated in the state that issued it, which is generally the Secretary of State's office. Federal documents go to the Department of State's Office of Authentications, and [the Convention's authority list](https://www.hcch.net/en/states/authorities/details3/?aid=353) records both routes. Ask which one your document belongs to before you pay anyone.
2. **United Kingdom.** Issued by the Legalisation Office for documents whose signatures it can match against its records, at a published standard fee of 45 pounds plus delivery.
3. **Canada.** In force since 11 January 2024, with apostilles issued by [Global Affairs Canada](https://international.canada.ca/en/global-affairs/services/document-authentication/updates) for Canadian documents used abroad.
4. **Colombia.** In force since 30 January 2001, so a Colombian public document needs the apostille rather than the older consular chain.
5. **Georgia.** In force since 14 May 2007, which is why a civil registration certificate issued there can travel inside the Convention system.

China has been a party since 7 November 2023. For a family whose documents will be presented to a Chinese authority, that changed the route: a foreign public document now needs an apostille at origin rather than the consular legalisation that used to be required. The authority that asks for the document is still the one that decides what else it wants with it.

## Translations are a separate requirement

A translation is not a second copy of the same problem. It is a different test applied by a different body, and it fails for reasons an apostille never touches.

- The receiving authority usually sets the standard: which languages, whether a sworn or accredited translator must be used, and whether the translator's own signature must itself be certified.
- The order matters. Where a translator's signature has to be apostilled, the translation comes first and the certificate attaches to it, so doing them in the wrong sequence means paying twice.
- Register formats differ. A short form extract and a full certified copy are not the same document, and an authority that wants parent names recorded will reject the version that does not carry them.

This is a place to get the requirement in writing from the body that will read the file, rather than from whoever is selling you the fastest route. The [United Kingdom guidance](https://www.gov.uk/get-document-legalised) says exactly that on the question of legalisation, and the principle is the same one step later.

## The timeline nobody prices

Add the pieces up before you commit to a birth date, because each of them belongs to a different institution and none of them is working to your agency's schedule.

Registration of the birth, then the security of the certified copies, then the issuing authority's turnaround for an apostille, then the translator, then the receiving authority's own processing, then any court application that has to be filed inside a fixed window. In the United Kingdom a parental order application [must be made within six months of the birth](/articles/the-six-month-limit-on-a-uk-parental-order), so this paperwork chain is running inside a clock that does not stop for a postal delay.

The cost is also not in the quote. [One of the twelve cost categories that sit outside a package price](/articles/surrogacy-costs-that-sit-outside-a-quote) is registration, translation and apostille, and it is the category with the least competition, because the body charging you cannot be shopped around.

## What to ask, and in what order

Download the [destination research checklist](/tools/destination-research-checklist) and write each answer beside its question, next to the name of the office that gave it, since a verbal answer from an agency about what a registry requires is a prediction about somebody else's desk.

1. Which authority in this country issues the apostille for this particular document, and what is its published turnaround?
2. How many certified copies will the receiving authority want, and does any of them need the full record rather than the extract?
3. Which translator does the receiving authority accept, and must the translator's signature be certified as well?
4. Does anything in the chain have to happen inside a court deadline, and what happens if it does not?
5. What does each authority in this chain keep, and what will the next one accept while it holds it?

That fifth question is worth putting in writing before anything is handed over, because each authority in the chain keeps a different piece of the file. The certificate attaches to the document you sent rather than to a copy of it; a translation is usually made from a certified copy; and an application may be filed on a copy instead of the register entry itself. What the next counter will accept, in what form, and while the previous one still holds something, is for that counter to answer. Who carries the papers across a whole journey belongs to a longer page: [bringing your child home](/guides/bringing-your-child-home).

This page describes published procedures and the dates published instruments took effect. It is not advice about a particular document, and it does not predict what any registry, court or consulate will accept.
