Every article about surrogacy cost opens with a number, and almost every one of those numbers was written by somebody selling the service. That is the first thing to understand about budgeting for surrogacy: the totals you find are quotes, not measurements.

There is no audited or government cost dataset for surrogacy, and nothing counts what intended parents actually paid. So the starting point is not a total. It is what the money is made of: the components of a budget, where it sits before it is spent, what a quote habitually leaves out, and what to confirm with a named professional before you sign.

## What a surrogacy budget is made of

A surrogacy program is not one bill. It is several independent payees, on different schedules, under different documents. Thinking in components is what lets you compare two quotes at all.

| Component | What it covers | Who bills you | What makes it move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agency or program fee | Matching, screening coordination, case management, sometimes counselling logistics | Agency | Whether screening is done in house or outsourced, and what happens if a match falls through |
| Medical treatment | Fertility medication, monitoring, egg retrieval or transfer, laboratory work, and prenatal care for the pregnancy | Clinic, laboratory, pharmacy, obstetric provider | Number of transfer attempts, whether a donor egg or sperm is needed, and which insurance pays for what |
| Carrier-side costs | Her expenses, lost income where permitted, maternity clothing, travel, childcare, counselling, insurance | Reimbursed or paid through the arrangement | How the destination's law treats reimbursement versus payment, and what documentation is required |
| Legal fees | Drafting and negotiating the arrangement, parentage proceedings, separate counsel for each party | Lawyers, court filing fees | The state or country, how many parties need independent advice, and whether a pre-birth order is available |
| Escrow administration | Holding and disbursing funds on the agreed schedule | Escrow provider | Monthly fees, minimum balance, and who authorises each release |
| Contingency reserve | Loss, cycle failure, extended neonatal care, pregnancy complications | You | Whether the reserve is your own decision or someone else's requirement |
| Travel and stay | Trips for transfers, appointments, birth, and document appointments | Airlines, hotels, employers, family logistics | Whether you can travel, how long the stay has to run, and who goes with you |
| Documentation afterwards | Birth registration, passports, citizenship or naturalisation steps, translation and apostille | Registries, consulates, translators | Whether your home country recognises the order made abroad |

Two of these rows are the ones people underestimate. The contingency reserve is the cost that turns a budget into a guess. The documentation row is the one that appears after the birth, when the money set aside for it has already gone into the medical line.

For a legal parentage route in the United States, the [professional guidance on financial terms](https://adoptionart.org/assisted-reproduction/financial-terms-escrow-accounts/) is blunt that these are separate documents and separate conversations: whatever the intended parents and the carrier agree about money has to be written into the signed agreement in full, rather than left in an invoice or a message thread.

## What a quote usually leaves out

A quote is usually a package description, not a ledger, and the distance between a quoted number and a paid one is made of items nobody labels as surrogacy: a second transfer attempt, medication and monitoring billed by the clinic rather than the program, insurance for the carrier, an independent lawyer for her ([the academic review of US practice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11621256/) records that separate representation is recommended and, sometimes, mandated), court and filing costs, neonatal care that runs past the discharge date, whatever is still owed when a pregnancy ends early, the trip itself, and the documents that have to exist before anyone flies home.

[The explainer on the costs that sit outside a quote](/articles/surrogacy-costs-that-sit-outside-a-quote) takes those twelve categories apart one at a time and turns each into a written question for a named party. The [quote comparison tool](/tools/agency-quote-comparison) then puts two quotes side by side on the same nine cost lines.

## Why two agencies quote the same program differently

A gap of 25,000 dollars between two quotes is not a 25,000 dollar saving. Before a difference means anything, four things have to match:

1. **Scope.** Which of the components above are inside the fee, and which are pass-through costs billed by someone else.
2. **Attempts.** Whether the fee covers one transfer or several, and what a failed cycle costs.
3. **Refund terms.** Whether a fee is returned when a match dissolves, a screening fails, or a pregnancy does not result. Identical fees with different refund rules are not identical offers.
4. **Who is being paid.** An agency fee may include legal work in one program and exclude it in another, which shows up as a second invoice later.

There is a fifth reason, and it is the least comfortable one: quoted fees vary because providers set them differently for people who cannot compare them. That is why the verification step is a document review rather than a price conversation.

## How the money actually moves

The mechanism to understand is an escrow account, because it decides when you are exposed. Two propositions from the [professional guidance](https://adoptionart.org/assisted-reproduction/financial-terms-escrow-accounts/) set the timing: the account is typically funded before the first embryo transfer rather than before the birth, and the written agreement is what states how much money it has to hold.

When that money is released, whether the account is genuinely neutral, and what happens to a balance if a pregnancy ends are clause-level questions, and the explainer on [surrogacy escrow accounts and payment stages](/articles/surrogacy-escrow-accounts-and-payment-stages) works through them one at a time, including the questions to put to the agency, your lawyer and the escrow provider. Read that page before you read a quote. A quote can state a correct total and still answer none of those questions.

## Payment schedules and refund terms

A payment schedule tells you when your money becomes non-refundable. Read it against the calendar of medical events, because the two are usually written by different people.

Concretely, check:

- **What triggers each payment.** A date, a medication start, a transfer, a heartbeat at scan, a birth?
- **What you have paid at the point the arrangement can still collapse.** If most of the agency fee is due before matching completes, the risk sits with you.
- **What a refund is calculated on.** Whole fee, part fee, administrative deduction, or credit toward a future cycle?
- **Whether the refund is a promise or a mechanism.** A refund term with no escrow or held funds behind it depends on the provider staying solvent and willing.

## The figures that exist, and what kind of number they are

The published totals are of three kinds: a provider's own marketing figure, an academic threshold, and a regulator's expenses range.

**United States.** The figures this market repeats most often are a fertility clinic's own: [an average of 100,000 to 140,000 US dollars for surrogacy in 2025](https://www.illumefertility.com/fertility-blog/average-cost-of-surrogacy-in-the-united-states), rising to 250,000 dollars or more. The same page itemises an agency fee of 35,000 to 55,000 dollars, base compensation of 35,000 to 60,000 dollars, and legal costs of 10,000 to 25,000 dollars. No methodology is disclosed. It is a provider's marketing page, and it is quoted here as that.

A peer-reviewed review article states it as a threshold rather than a range: [US costs via a gestational carrier passed 100,000 dollars in 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11621256/). That is an academic citation of other people's sources rather than an original dataset, which is roughly the state of the evidence.

**United Kingdom.** The regulator's factsheet puts the usual reimbursement at [10,000 to 15,000 pounds](https://www.hfea.gov.uk/about-us/media-centre/surrogacy-a-factsheet/), a figure it takes from a report by Surrogacy UK. That is an expenses range rather than a program cost, and no verified UK total exists to plan against. Anything above reasonable expenses needs family court authorisation before a parental order can be made, so the number the law cares about is the expenses line rather than the sum.

**Canada.** Canadian law governs what may be reimbursed rather than what things cost. [Health Canada's guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/programs/consultation-reimbursement-assisted-human-reproduction/document.html) enumerates reimbursable expenditures, including travel, childcare, counselling, legal fees, medications, maternity clothing, prenatal classes, delivery, insurance, and lost work-related income during the pre-pregnancy and post-partum period. Receipts are required and records must be kept for six years.

What none of these figures is: a measurement of what you will pay, or an average across real programs. The reasons are set out in [Why there is no average cost of surrogacy](/articles/why-no-average-surrogacy-cost-exists).

Every figure above, and the source behind it, is laid out line by line on [the published cost figures page](/tools/cost-figures).

## What to verify before you pay anything

Work through this list against documents rather than conversations. If an answer only exists verbally, it is not yet an answer.

- [ ] A written agreement containing the complete financial terms, not an invoice plus a package brochure.
- [ ] Every line of the quote mapped to one of the components above, with a named payee.
- [ ] What is excluded, stated as exclusions rather than discovered later.
- [ ] The escrow amount, funding date, disbursement authority and administration fee.
- [ ] Refund terms for each failure point: no match, failed screening, failed cycle, loss, and birth.
- [ ] Whether the carrier has independent legal advice, and who pays for it.
- [ ] Insurance arrangements for the carrier and the child, and which policy covers a premature birth.
- [ ] The parentage route in the specific state or country, and whether a lawyer admitted there has confirmed it for your facts.
- [ ] The documentation needed to bring the child home, and who is responsible for obtaining it.
- [ ] A contingency reserve you have decided on, sized against the exclusions rather than the brochure.

None of this is legal, medical or financial advice. Cost structure and payment mechanics are set by the documents of your own arrangement, and the totals depend on your jurisdiction, your medical path, and the providers you engage. A figure or a rule that is not in one of the sources above needs a named professional to confirm it, not an assumption.
