A refund guarantee is the part of a surrogacy proposal that reads most like insurance, and the part where that reading costs the most. It is not insurance. It is rarely a promise about a child. It is a set of conditions attached to money you have already paid, written by the party that benefits if the conditions are never met.

The useful way to read one is to look for three things, in this order: the trigger, the unit and the source. A clause that answers two of the three is not a guarantee. It is a sentence that will be argued about at the worst possible moment, by people who no longer have an incentive to agree with you.

## Three words, and where each has to be written

| The word | The question it settles | Where the answer has to appear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | What has to happen before any money moves back | The guarantee clause itself, naming the document that proves the event |
| Unit | Which fees come back, and which never do | An itemised list, using the same names as the payment schedule |
| Source | Which account the money leaves from | The escrow agreement, the insurance policy, or the agency's own terms |

### The trigger

Words like "unsuccessful cycle" and "no live birth" are not triggers until the document says who certifies them. A cycle that ends in a loss, a transfer cancelled before it happens, and a course of treatment a clinician stops are three different events, and a clause written for one of them will not pay out on the other two.

Ask what the certification consists of: which clinician or which record, how many days after the event, and who has to be told. An event that nobody is empowered to certify is not a trigger. It is a hope.

### The unit

A guarantee is usually attached to one line of the budget and not to the others. The lines that habitually come back are the programme or agency fee. The lines that habitually do not are the medical fees already incurred and the carrier's compensation already earned. The least refundable line in the document is often the premium you paid for the guarantee itself.

Do not accept a percentage without a base. Seventy per cent of what, priced at the amount in the schedule or at the amount in the invoice? On a proposal with a rising payment schedule those two numbers are not the same.

### The source

Money promised from three different places carries three different reliabilities, and only one of them is really an escrow.

1. **A third-party insurer.** The obligation sits with the insurer, and the document that governs you is the policy, not the agency's page. Read the exclusions, because they decide.
2. **An escrow balance you funded.** The money already exists and is not the agency's to spend. Read who authorises release, which is a separate question from who promises the refund.
3. **The agency's own funds.** This is a credit risk dressed as a guarantee. It may be perfectly sound. It is still a claim on a business, and a business can close.

## Four shapes worth being able to name

Programmes describe these in their own words, and the words are not interchangeable. Naming which one you are being offered is most of the work.

1. **A tiered refund.** A share comes back before treatment starts, a smaller share after a pregnancy is confirmed, none after a live birth. Read it as a schedule against events, not as a single percentage.
2. **An attempt package.** Nothing is refunded, and another transfer or another cycle is owed instead. Check the conditions on the second attempt: the same clinic, a time limit, and a list of reasons that void it.
3. **A carrier replacement.** What is owed is a new match, not money. The cost and the calendar of that second match are usually excluded, and both are real.
4. **An insured product.** The agency sells it, someone else underwrites it. Everything that decides a claim lives in a policy you may not have been handed yet.

Which of the four a given programme offers is a question for that programme. What this page can say is that they are not variants of one thing, and a proposal that names none of them has not answered the question.

## Five questions that make a guarantee executable

Ask the agency, the escrow provider and your own lawyer separately, then compare. Where the three answers differ, you have found the clause to rewrite.

1. **Who certifies the trigger, and from which record?** A named role and a named document, not "the medical team".
2. **Which lines are refunded, and which are excluded in writing?** If the exclusion list is shorter than the refund list, ask why.
3. **Which account does the money come from, who holds it, and who authorises a release?** If one party can both promise and withhold, the promise is decoration.
4. **If a refund and another attempt both exist, which applies first, and can they be combined?** Most documents make you choose, and most readers do not notice until they must.
5. **What happens if the programme stops rather than fails?** An agency closing, a clinic losing its licence, a country changing its rules: three events a guarantee written around a failed cycle may not cover at all.

## What a guarantee cannot do

It cannot tell you whether the business behind it will still be there when the trigger fires. It cannot survive a contract that is unenforceable where you signed it, which is a question for the jurisdiction and for [the documents signed before a first transfer](/guides/documents-before-embryo-transfer). It cannot bind the carrier, the clinic or an underwriter to a decision they are entitled to make. And it cannot change a medical outcome.

A guarantee is a clause about money after an event. It is not a mechanism for producing the event you wanted, and a proposal that presents it as one is selling a certainty it does not hold.

## What to have in hand before comparing two

Four documents. A brochure sentence is not one of them.

- The guarantee clause as it appears in the agreement, not as it appears in the proposal.
- The escrow terms and the account details, where escrow is involved.
- The full policy, where the guarantee is insured.
- A written refund process: who submits a claim, to whom, within how many days, and what happens when no answer comes.

Only the first can be enforced. The other three decide whether enforcing it is worth the effort.

## Questions people ask

**If the proposal says 100% refund, is there no risk left?** Ask one hundred per cent of what. It is usually one line of the budget, under conditions, and often the smallest line. The test is not the percentage. It is whether the trigger can be certified by someone who is not the party being asked to pay.

**Can the guarantee premium and the medical fees come back together?** Usually not, and the reason is not malice. Medical fees have gone to third parties for work that was done. The premium is a separate product with its own terms, and its refundability is set by those terms rather than by the medical outcome.

**The agency changed its name or was acquired. Does the guarantee travel with it?** Read who the counterparty is, and whether the obligation can be assigned. The name on the letterhead is not always the party that owes you the money. A guarantee is only as good as the entity standing behind it on the day it is called.
