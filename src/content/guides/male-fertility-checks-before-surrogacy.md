A carrier's file arrives thick: screening, bloodwork, imaging, a psychological assessment, a report from her own doctor, a signed consent for each step. The intended father's file often arrives with one report, or with nothing at all, and nobody in the room notices, because the pregnancy is not going to happen in his body.

That is the whole explanation. The arrangement is organised around the person who will carry, so the person who will not carry is organised around later. The gap is not usually a decision anyone made. It is a question nobody owned.

## Why the file stays thin

Four reasons, and they compound:

- **The question feels already answered.** The carrier is the one being treated, so her results are the ones on the table. His are treated as background.
- **He is not the patient of record.** The clinic's patient is the carrier. A clinic does not chase test results from someone who is not its patient, and the man may not think of himself as anyone's patient at all.
- **The sample is taken somewhere else.** A collection arranged near home, a report filed, a cycle planned months later. Nobody returns to the file to see whether the plan still fits it.
- **Ownership is split.** The agency coordinates, the clinic treats, the lawyer documents. Each does its part, and the question of whether the male half is complete falls between all three.

## What actually depends on it

This page does not say which measurement changes which treatment. That is a clinical judgement, and one that depends on results it cannot see. What can be described is where a decision waits on a record that may not exist.

| Decision | What it waits on | Where the gap surfaces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whether the arrangement uses his gametes or a donor's | A result, read by a clinician, before the plan is fixed | Late, while the cycle is already being scheduled |
| Whether the clinic wants further investigation before a transfer | A first result that exists to investigate from | At the clinic, sometimes on the day of the appointment |
| What the consent documents describe | An accurate description of the source | At signing, when the wording is already drafted |
| What an insurer or a contract treats as known beforehand | A dated record | When a claim or a dispute is argued |

In each row the cost of the gap is the same: a decision made on less information than was available, and at the latest possible moment. That is a planning problem before it is a medical one.

## What a semen analysis is not

- **Not a verdict.** It describes a sample, produced on a day, read by a laboratory. It is not a statement about whether a pregnancy will happen.
- **Not the same question as a transfer.** Whether a transfer works depends on the embryo, the carrier's uterus and timing. His result is upstream of that, and it does not predict it.
- **Not stable by definition.** One sample describes one sample. Two collections can be read differently, which is why a clinic may want its own.
- **Not interchangeable between laboratories.** The method and the reference range belong to the laboratory that produced them, which makes the report the thing to bring, not the number.

None of that makes the test unimportant. It makes it a document rather than a score, and documents can be checked.

## Who should be asking

| Party | What it should be asking | What it will not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clinic | Which results it needs, from whom, by when, and whether it will accept one produced elsewhere | Chase a man who is not its patient of record |
| Agency | Whether the file is complete for matching and for its own fee schedule | Interpret a result, or say what it means |
| Lawyer | Whether the documents correctly describe the source of the gametes | Obtain the test, or assess it |

The middle row is where most of the confusion lives. An agency that says the medical side is handled is describing a boundary, not a guarantee, and the boundary is worth writing down: which party confirms that his file is complete, and by which date.

## Four questions before the first consultation

- Whose results does the clinic need from him, and does it accept results produced at home?
- Which decisions would change if his record said something different?
- If a result is missing on the day, does the appointment proceed, and what is discussed instead?
- If the arrangement ends up using donor gametes, whose decision is that, and what has to be signed before it is made?

The second question is the one people skip, and it is the one that tells you whether the file matters. A clinic that cannot name a decision which depends on his results has told you the file is being collected for the record rather than for the plan.

## The same question, from the other side

The folder you bring to a first appointment has a history of its own: results produced by different laboratories, at different times, for different questions. [What happens to the fertility tests you arrive with](/guides/fertility-tests-at-a-new-clinic) sets out how a clinic sorts that folder, and why the male half is usually the thinner one.

## What this does not solve

Whether his result needs treatment, and what treatment, is a clinical question that belongs to a consultation with a doctor who has the report. This page describes who should be asking for the record and when, not what the record should say.

It also cannot tell you whether a particular clinic takes the male side seriously. That is visible only in the answers it gives to the questions above, which is why they are worth asking in writing.

## Questions people ask

**Does the intended father need testing if the arrangement uses donor sperm?**

Sometimes yes, for reasons unrelated to the donor question, and that is the clinic's call. The useful question is not whether he needs a test in general but which decisions his own record would change.

**Can his results be done near home?**

Often the collection can, and whether the destination clinic accepts the result is the earlier question. Ask which laboratory it will accept before arranging anything.

**Is a fertility check-up the same thing?**

No. A check-up is a set of questions a clinic asks a couple; a semen analysis is a single measurement. The first can be complete while the second has never been done, and only one of them appears in a file.
