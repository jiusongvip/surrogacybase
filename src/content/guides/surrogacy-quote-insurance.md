A surrogacy quote is an inventory. It lists work: screening, medication, the transfer, the carrier's fee, the legal file. Every line has a number, the numbers add up, and the total fits in a column.

Insurance is not an inventory. It is a set of promises about events that may never happen, and what it pays is decided after the event, by an office that was not in the room when you signed. That is why a quote can be complete and still describe only half of what an arrangement costs. The quote ends when the treatment ends. The bills that matter most tend to arrive later than that.

Nothing below states what is covered where. Coverage is a matter of a policy number, a country and a date, and a general guide that named figures would be inventing them. What can be given is the structure of the question, and the party who owes each answer.

## Three policies, and none of them is the quote

In most arrangements three separate covers are in play, and they are connected only loosely.

- **The carrier's own health cover.** Held by her, written before you entered the picture, and governed by whatever her policy says about pregnancy, about surrogacy, and about who pays for what.
- **A policy written for the pregnancy.** Sometimes arranged by the programme, sometimes by the carrier, and often the line a quote labels simply as insurance.
- **Cover for the child.** Starts, if it starts at all, at a moment the policy states: at birth, at discharge, on application, or on some later date.

The three rarely share an insurer, a start date, or a definition of what counts as a complication. So a question about insurance is first a question about which of the three you are asking, and a quote that answers with one word is answering about none of them.

## The window that opens at birth

Before the birth there is one patient, and the patient is the carrier. After the birth there are two, and one of them has no policy history, no member number, and in many arrangements no file at all.

Whether that changes anything, and from which hour, is written in the policy rather than in the quote. Ask for the sentence instead of the summary: the clause that says when the child's cover begins and what has to have happened for it to apply. A program that answers "the baby is covered" has answered a marketing question. The answer worth having contains a date, a condition and a document you can point at.

## What a quote leaves out by design

A quote is built from the things its author controls. Premiums, deductibles and exclusions are usually controlled by somebody else, which is the ordinary reason they are missing. The lines worth reconciling against any proposal:

- Who pays the premium, for how many months, and what happens if the pregnancy runs past the term that was quoted.
- The deductible and any co-insurance, and whether the programme or the escrow account absorbs them.
- Exclusions that matter here rather than in general: a multiple pregnancy, a preterm delivery, a condition the carrier already had, care taken outside the network the policy uses.
- What the carrier's own policy says about a pregnancy she is carrying for someone else, which is the clause most likely to surprise both of you.
- What happens if a claim is declined. Not whether the policy is generous, but who is holding money when the answer is no.

Read the last one first. Every other line tells you how the arrangement is meant to work. That line tells you who pays when it does not.

## One page, four columns

The exercise that makes this concrete takes an afternoon. Four columns, one row per event:

| Event | Who pays | Which document says so | Who signed it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carrier screening | | | |
| Medication and monitoring | | | |
| The transfer | | | |
| A hospital admission before viability | | | |
| Delivery | | | |
| Care for the child after birth | | | |
| A stay that runs longer than planned | | | |

The first two columns are what everyone discusses. The last two are what decides the argument. A party can describe a payment on a call and then be unable to name the clause, the document or the person who agreed to it, and that inability is itself the finding. Where two documents disagree, you have located a question for a lawyer rather than for the agency.

## Five questions, and who owes each

| Question | Party to ask | What a usable answer contains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which policy covers the carrier during the pregnancy, and what does it exclude? | The carrier's insurer, through the agency | A policy number and the exclusion clause, not a summary |
| When does the child's cover begin, and on what condition? | Whoever wrote that policy | The clause, plus the date it took effect |
| What are the premium, deductible and co-insurance, and who pays them? | The programme | A schedule with payees |
| If a claim is declined, who pays, and from which account? | The programme and the escrow administrator | A named account and the release terms |
| Which of these can I read before I sign? | All of the above | Copies, not descriptions |

Ask the fourth question twice, once of the programme and once of the escrow administrator, and compare. Where the answers differ, the difference is the gap this page is about.

## A fund is not a policy

A contingency reserve in a quote is a budget, and a budget is a number you spend. A policy transfers a bill you cannot bound to a third party. Both have a place, and one does not substitute for the other: a reserve sized against a typical outcome stops being a reserve the first time the outcome is not typical.

That distinction also shows up in how a proposal is written. A quote that lists insurance as a line item has priced an administration cost. It has not told you who answers for a hospital stay, which is the question the line appears to answer.

## What this does not solve

Which policies exist in a given country, what they exclude, and whether you can buy cover as a person who is not a resident there: none of that is settled by a general page, and the answer changes with the policy year. Your own legal and financial position is also not something a guide can read.

What is settled here is the shape of the question and the name of the party who owes an answer, and the order to ask them in: the clause first, the total afterwards.

## Questions people ask

**Does the insurance line in a quote cover the baby?**

The line usually names a policy rather than a scope. Ask for the clause that begins the child's cover and the date it is written against. Until you have that, a line labelled insurance has not told you anything about the child.

**Can we arrange cover ourselves instead?**

Sometimes, and the timing decides whether it is possible at all. Whether an application can be made, by whom, and at which point it stops being accepted are questions for an insurer and for the country involved, and they are worth asking before the transfer rather than after a bill arrives.

**Is a larger contingency reserve the simpler answer?**

It answers the bill and not the question. A reserve absorbs a cost you have already been charged. It does not tell you who was obliged to pay it, and the obligation is what a policy, an escrow term or a contract is for.
