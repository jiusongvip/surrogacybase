A failed transfer arrives as a verdict when it is only an outcome. The appointment where it is announced is often the worst possible moment to decide anything, because the two questions that matter have not been separated: did this arrangement do something wrong, and would another arrangement do better.

The first question is answered by the record. The second is answered by a clinic's reasoning about that record. Neither is answered by how the news was delivered, which is what everyone remembers.

## What a failed transfer is evidence of

It is evidence that this transfer did not produce a pregnancy. That is all it is evidence of on its own. It is not evidence of a mistake, of a badly chosen clinic, or of a decision you got wrong, and treating it as any of those is how people end up paying twice for the same outcome with a different letterhead.

The information that separates an outcome from a fault sits in the cycle's record: what was transferred, how it was prepared for, what the laboratory observed, what was known before the transfer and what was discovered during it. If you do not have that record, you do not yet have a basis for a decision.

## What the clinic owes you first

Before any discussion of changing anything, four things should be available. A clinic that provides them is not necessarily competent, but a clinic that withholds them cannot be evaluated at all.

- **A review with the record present.** Not a summary of how you feel, and not a statistic about averages: what happened in this cycle, in order.
- **An account of what it would do differently, and why.** A hypothesis, with the evidence behind it.
- **The laboratory's own record of the embryo.** How it was graded, on which day, and what was observed in culture.
- **A statement of what remains unexplained.** Naming the gap is a sign the reasoning is real. A clinic that can explain everything after the fact is describing hindsight, not knowledge.

Ask for these in writing and keep them. They are also the documents the next clinic will ask for, and the difference between arriving with a record and arriving with a story is the difference between a consultation and a sales meeting.

## Signals that belong to the clinic

| Signal | What it suggests | Would another clinic change it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No review, or a review that avoids the record | The file may not exist in a usable form | Possibly, if the record is still obtainable |
| The plan changed on the day, with no explanation recorded | Decisions taken downstream of the plan | Possibly, if the reason was recorded somewhere |
| You cannot obtain your own results | The file is treated as the clinic's property | Yes, and this one is worth acting on alone |
| The laboratory cannot describe the embryo's history | Storage and grading records are thin | Yes |
| Asked what it would do differently, it says nothing | The clinic has no next hypothesis to offer | Yes |

The third row deserves its own sentence. In most jurisdictions you have a right of access to your own medical record, and a clinic that makes that difficult is telling you something about how it will behave when a harder question arrives.

## Signals that belong to the case

Some of what happened is a property of the case rather than of the clinic: the embryo that was available, how the carrier's own cycle responded, the timing the calendar allowed, the stage the arrangement had reached, and plain chance. Changing clinic changes none of them.

The distinction matters because the two kinds of signal produce the same feeling. A clinic can be excellent and still produce this outcome, and a clinic can be poor and produce a success. That is exactly why the decision should rest on the record and on the reasoning about it, not on the outcome alone.

## What changing actually costs

Four costs, and the first two are the ones people underestimate.

1. **The calendar.** A new clinic repeats screening and books its own appointments. The carrier may need to be re-cleared, which is measured in weeks rather than days.
2. **The money.** The fee schedule does not travel. Parts already spent are spent, and what is refundable depends on the service agreement, not on the outcome. [Surrogacy escrow and payment stages](/guides/surrogacy-escrow-accounts-and-payment-stages) sets out why the timing of each payment decides what a change actually costs.
3. **The carrier.** She agreed to work with a clinic, not with clinics in the abstract. A change may require her consent, and the agency's, and her willingness is not a formality.
4. **The legal trail.** The clinic's record is part of how the birth is documented later. Moving mid-arrangement means two records and a handover, and the handover is a document somebody has to write.

Add a fifth if the agency and the clinic are the same company: changing one then changes both, and the arrangement you are evaluating may no longer exist in the same form.

## Four questions for the next clinic

- What in our record would have changed your plan, and what would it have changed it to?
- What will you repeat, at whose cost, before you would transfer again?
- What would make you decline this case?
- Who writes the handover, and what happens if the two records disagree?

The last question is the one that reveals whether the clinic has done this before. A clinic that has no answer has never received a case from another clinic, which is worth knowing before you become the first.

## What this does not solve

Whether changing clinics is the right clinical decision belongs to a consultation with a doctor who has read the record, and possibly to a second opinion obtained deliberately. This page describes what to obtain before deciding and what the change costs, not whether to make it.

It also cannot tell you whether the first clinic was at fault. That question has a legal form and, in most places, a complaints route that exists precisely because the answer is not available from a website.

## Questions people ask

**Is one failed transfer enough to change clinics?**

There is no rule, and a page that supplied one would be inventing it. The useful test is not the number of failures but whether the record explains what happened. An explanation you can evaluate is worth more than a fresh start you cannot.

**Will a new clinic tell us the last one got it wrong?**

Rarely in writing, and a clinic that criticises a competitor too readily is telling you something about how it will describe you later. The question that produces usable information is not whether the last clinic was wrong but what this one would do differently.

**Does changing the clinic change the surrogacy agreement?**

It can. The agreement usually names a clinic, sometimes a country, and often a fee schedule tied to both. Read the replacement clause before you read the new clinic's brochure, and check whose consent the clause requires.
