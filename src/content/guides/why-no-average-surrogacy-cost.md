Search for the cost of surrogacy and you will find a number within the first two results. Ask where it comes from and the answer is usually another article quoting a clinic.

That is not a coincidence or laziness. It reflects something structural that almost nobody explains: **there is no dataset of what surrogacy costs, anywhere.** Not in the United States, not in the United Kingdom, and not internationally. So the number at the top of the page is either a provider's own price framing, or a citation of one.

## What the registries actually count

The closest thing to national infrastructure in the United States is the Society for Assisted Reproduction Clinic Outcome Reporting System. It is substantial: [more than 90 percent of US clinics providing assisted reproduction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9722991/) contribute cycle data covering cycle starts, patient demographics and linked cycles.

But it counts **treatment cycles**, not surrogacy programs. There is no element for the agreement, none for compensation, none for what an agency charged, and none for what the intended parents spent in total or what happened to the money when a cycle failed.

That distinction matters, because registry data is what makes an honest average possible in other areas of medicine. Researchers have used this same registry to describe surrogate treatment: of [40,177 embryo transfers to a surrogate from 2014 to 2020, 32 percent were for intended parents living outside the United States](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10978240/). That is a real, defensible statistic about a *clinical* activity. There is no equivalent for the commercial and legal half of the journey, because nobody collects it.

## What the published figures are

Take the most commonly cited US number. A fertility clinic's page, published February 2025 and updated August 2025, states that the [average cost of surrogacy in the US in 2025 ranges from 100,000 to 140,000 US dollars](https://www.illumefertility.com/fertility-blog/average-cost-of-surrogacy-in-the-united-states) and can rise to 250,000 dollars or more.

Read it as what it is: a provider's description of prices at providers, with no disclosed methodology, no sample, and no definition of what is inside the number. It is genuinely useful as a market signal. It is not a measurement.

Even the academic literature is explicit about its own limits. The peer-reviewed review that states the cost of having a baby via a surrogate in the US [surpassed 100,000 dollars in 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11621256/) is citing other sources rather than presenting primary cost data. That is as good as the published evidence gets, and it is honest about being second-hand.

The UK is a different case rather than a better one. The regulator reports that surrogates are typically reimbursed [10,000 to 15,000 pounds](https://www.hfea.gov.uk/about-us/media-centre/surrogacy-a-factsheet/), citing a report by the organisation Surrogacy UK. That figure exists because UK law limits payments to reasonable expenses, so the number being estimated is a legally defined category rather than an undefined market. There is no verified UK total-cost figure at all.

## Why an average could not work even if the data existed

A surrogacy total is not one price with variation around it. It is the sum of independent payees, several of which are contingent on medical events:

- A package that covers one transfer and a package that covers three are different products with the same name.
- Whether a donor egg is needed changes the medical line more than any fee negotiation.
- Legal costs move with the state, and with whether that state will make the order before the birth.
- Neonatal care for a premature infant is a hospital bill, not a surrogacy fee.
- Refund terms decide how much of a paid fee you keep when a program stops.

Averaging across that distribution produces a number that describes no actual program. The mean of two quotes is meaningless when their scopes differ; this is the arithmetic reason the [comparison tool](/tools/agency-quote-comparison) refuses to treat a gap between totals as a cost difference until every line's scope is settled.

## What to use instead of an average

1. **Build the budget from components.** A list of payees, each with a named biller, is a budget. One borrowed number is a hope. The [cost guide](/guides/surrogacy-cost) sets out the components and the contingent items.
2. **Take the exclusions seriously.** The items outside a quote usually determine the final total. See [the costs that sit outside a quote](/guides/surrogacy-costs-outside-a-quote).
3. **Price the risk you carry.** How many attempts you agree to fund, and what happens to the plan if none of them produces a child, moves your number further than any market average can.
4. **Ask for the escrow schedule.** When the money must be in an account, and what happens to it, is more predictive of your exposure than the headline fee. See [escrow accounts and payment stages](/guides/surrogacy-escrow-accounts-and-payment-stages).
5. **Label every figure you write down** with who published it, when, and whether they sell the service. If you cannot label it, do not put it in the budget. The [published cost figures](/tools/cost-figures) page lists the numbers that arrive with that labelling already attached: each row names the source that published it and the year those figures describe.

That is more work than reading a headline number, and it is the only version that survives contact with an actual invoice.

What is described here is how surrogacy cost data gets produced. It is not financial advice, and no figure here is a quote for your situation.
