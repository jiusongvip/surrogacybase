Most intended parents arrive at a first consultation carrying a folder. It holds blood results, a scan report, a semen analysis dated a year ago, a letter from a doctor who is no longer treating them, and a summary somebody typed. The folder is usually treated as an asset, as evidence of diligence.

A clinic reads it differently. It asks who produced each result, by which method, on which date, for which question, and whether the laboratory that produced it is one it can rely on. The folder is not a record of your fertility. It is a set of claims, each with an author and a date.

## What a result carries

| Element | Why the clinic asks | What to have ready |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The method | Two laboratories can report the same quantity on different scales | The report itself, not a summary of it |
| The date | A result describes a moment, not a standing property | The collection date, not the date it was printed |
| The author and the laboratory | Someone has to be answerable for the number | The letterhead, and the laboratory's name |
| The question it was ordered for | A test run for one reason may not answer another | Why your own doctor ordered it |

A result without its method is a number without units. A result without its date is a claim about a person at no particular time. Neither is what a clinic is being asked to plan a cycle around, and yet both arrive in folders every week.

## Three things that can happen to a result

Nothing on this page says which test is required, at what age, or how often. That is a clinical judgement, and a page that supplied an interval would be inventing one. What can be described is the handling, because there are only three outcomes:

- **Accepted as it stands.** Usually a report from a laboratory the clinic already works with, or one signed by a specialist who will remain involved.
- **Repeated for the clinic's own record.** Its own laboratory, its own standard, its own date, because the result will be cited in its decision later.
- **Set aside.** When the original question was different, or the method has since changed, or nobody can say who produced it.

Which of the three your file will meet is a question for the clinic before you book anything, and the answer is more useful in an email than in the last five minutes of a consultation.

## The date is part of the result

A result is a photograph of a state. It is not a property that the person carries. Two consequences follow, and both cost money if you meet them late.

The first is that a value may have moved between the report and the cycle. Whether that matters, and what the clinic does about it, is its decision to make and to state.

The second is that a result may be too old to be acted on even though nothing about it is wrong. How old is too old is not a universal number. Ask the clinic for its rule, in writing, and ask what it does when a value sits exactly on the boundary.

## What the agency asks for, and what it does not

An agency usually asks for identity documents, insurance, sometimes a medical summary written by your own doctor. It rarely asks for the laboratory reports themselves, and it is not the party that reads them. That is not a criticism: matching a carrier and pricing a fee do not require a laboratory.

It matters only when the two roles are confused. If an agency offers a view on your results, ask who is giving that view, in what capacity, and whether it is willing to put the view in writing. An opinion that cannot be attributed is not evidence about your file, however reassuring it sounds.

## The half that arrives thin

In most folders one partner has more pages than the other, and the reason is structural rather than personal: the pregnancy will not happen in the second partner's body, so nobody organises the file around him. That asymmetry has its own consequences, set out in [the half of a fertility file that arrives empty](/guides/male-fertility-checks-before-surrogacy).

## Five questions to put in writing

| Question | Who answers | What counts as an answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which results will you accept as they stand, and which will you repeat? | Clinic | A list, with a reason beside each repeat |
| Whose laboratory has to have produced the result? | Clinic | A named standard, or the name of a laboratory it accepts |
| How old may a result be before you repeat it? | Clinic | A stated interval, and what happens at the boundary |
| If a result is repeated and the two differ, which one goes in the record? | Clinic | A stated rule, not a preference |
| Who pays for the repeats, and are they inside the quoted price? | Clinic and agency | A line in the fee schedule, or an admission that there is none |

The last question is the one that turns a clinical answer into a budget line. Repeats are ordinary, and a quote that assumed none of them was written by somebody who had not read the folder. [What a surrogacy quote assumes about insurance](/guides/surrogacy-quote-insurance) makes the same point about the cover behind the price.

## What this does not solve

Whether any particular test is appropriate for you, and what a value means, are clinical questions. This page describes how a result is handled once it exists, not what it should say. It also cannot tell you whether a clinic's standard is a good one, because that requires the laboratory's accreditation and the clinic's own record, neither of which is visible from outside.

What is settled here is narrower and still useful: a result is a claim with an author, a method and a date, and every one of the three can be asked about before a cycle is booked.

## Questions people ask

**Should the tests be done at home or at the destination?**

Both happen. At home is usually cheaper and quicker, and it keeps the result with the doctor who ordered it. The destination clinic may repeat part of it anyway, so the question to settle first is which results it will accept, and from which laboratory.

**The agency says our file is complete. Is it?**

Complete for the agency's purpose, which is matching and pricing, is not the same as complete for a clinic's. The two lists overlap and neither contains the other.

**Do results expire?**

A clinic sets the interval, and the interval can vary by test. Ask for the rule rather than for a number, because the rule tells you what happens when a value sits on the boundary.
