The medical part of a program ends at a birth. The part that decides whether your family travels home together starts there, and it belongs to institutions nobody in your arrangement employs: a court in the state of birth, a civil registry, a consulate, a passport office, and, for a child who will grow up in China, a local public security sub-office. None of them is a party to your contract, and none of them works to a schedule anyone selling you a program can influence.

This page follows that chain in the order it has to be done. Two of its links have pages of their own and are not repeated here: [how an apostille works and who issues one](/articles/getting-a-birth-certificate-accepted-abroad), and [the six-month limit on a UK parental order](/articles/the-six-month-limit-on-a-uk-parental-order).

One point of scope before the chain begins. China appears on this page as the country a family returns to, not as a place to arrange a birth: the administrative measures cited below set penalties for the institutions that carry out surrogacy there, so this site publishes no destination record for China. The United States and the United Kingdom appear because their courts, registries and consulates issue the documents a child needs, and each of them has a record on the destinations page.

## Three documents, three different authorities

A child born through an arrangement usually has to hold three things at once before the family can move.

- A record of the birth, issued by the authority in the place where the baby was born.
- A decision on who the child's legal parents are, issued by a court or registry in a system that will recognise it.
- A travel document the child can cross a border on, which some authority has agreed to issue.

Each comes from a different institution with its own turnaround, and the order is not negotiable: the second usually needs the first, and the third needs both.

The third document is the one that surprises families most. The assumption is that a baby travels on the passport of the country of birth. In a family whose parents are Chinese nationals that is frequently not what happens, because a consulate decides a question about the child's own nationality before it issues anything.

## The parentage order is a separate step

[California Courts' own self-help guide](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/parentage/surrogacy) states that a party still has to ask the court for a final decision, called a judgment, about who the child's legal parents are, even where the gestational agreement itself is valid. The forms differ by whether everyone agrees:

- A joint petition on form SUR-100(J) where the intended parents, the surrogate and her spouse or partner if she has one are all in agreement.
- A contested petition on form SUR-100 where someone is not.
- A judgment on form FL-250, with an accompanying FL-190.

That is a court step, so it means a filing, a hearing or an order on the papers, and a wait set by the court's calendar. It also means the parentage answer arrives after the birth, which leaves the identity document in the next two sections being applied for on the basis of an order that may not exist yet.

California is named because it publishes the procedure. Nothing here describes another state's rules, and your own jurisdiction's lawyer is the one to ask which order that system requires.

## Where a genetic link is demanded, and where it is not

Three of the systems this page touches ask about biology, and they ask it at different counters.

- **The United Kingdom** grants a parental order only where at least one parent has a genetic link to the child, according to [the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office's guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/surrogacy-overseas/surrogacy-overseas); the same page requires at least one intended parent to be domiciled in the United Kingdom and the application to be made after the child arrives.
- **The United States**, for a child born abroad to a United States citizen, asks that [at least one parent show a genetic or gestational relationship](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/legal/travel-legal-considerations/us-citizenship/US-Citizenship-DNA-Testing.html), and the same page warns that a submission does not guarantee a passport, a Consular Report of Birth Abroad or an immigrant visa.
- **China** asks at the registration counter rather than the consular one: [a published local service guide](https://gc.jiceng.zhengzhou.gov.cn/004001/3543190.jhtml) requires a paternity report from a qualified domestic institution where parentage is not established on the record, while the consular document checklists read for this page ask for no genetic testing at all.

The United Kingdom's rule is the strictest of the three because it closes a route rather than delaying it: where neither intended parent is genetically related to the child, a parental order is not available. The American formulation is wider on its face, since it accepts a gestational relationship as well.

The Chinese one is the most likely to be missing from a plan, because the demand arrives late and from a domestic institution. A report produced by an institute that the registration authority recognises is not something you can commission at short notice from the other side of the world.

## A travel document or a visa: the test is about the parents

[China's Nationality Law](https://www.nia.gov.cn/n741440/n741547/c1013967/content.html) decides this, and Article 5 states the rule in one sentence: where both parents or one of them are Chinese citizens and the child is born abroad, the child has Chinese nationality. The exception sits in the same sentence: where the Chinese-national parent is settled abroad and the child acquires foreign nationality at birth, the child does not have Chinese nationality.

The consulates apply that literally, and what they check is the parents' residence status, not the baby's. [The Los Angeles consulate's checklist](https://losangeles.china-consulate.gov.cn/lbqw/lszj/hzlxz/202506/t20250625_11658204.htm) requires that the Chinese-national parent held no permanent residence in the United States or any other country at the time of the birth, and states that a child born to parents who do not meet that condition applies for a Chinese visa to travel to China instead. So the two branches are:

- A child the consulate treats as a Chinese national does not enter China on a visa at all. That child travels on [China's consular travel document](https://us.china-embassy.gov.cn/chn/lsfw/zj/hzlxz/202204/t20220416_10668741.htm), the document issued to a Chinese citizen who has no passport in hand.
- A child whose parents held permanent residence at the time of birth is treated as a foreign national and enters on [a Chinese visa in the ordinary way](https://us.china-embassy.gov.cn/lsfw/zj/qz/201502/t20150205_5098662.htm).

The embassy's document list adds one item for the first branch: a child born in the United States to Chinese-national parents who were not settled there supplies the child's birth certificate along with the standard set, and both passport and travel document applications now run through China's consular affairs application.

Which branch your family sits on was fixed at the moment of birth. A permanent residence card obtained afterwards does not move it, and nothing the child decides later moves it either.

## What the nationality rules leave open

Article 3 of the same law states that the People's Republic of China does not recognise Chinese citizens holding dual nationality. Article 9 states that a Chinese citizen settled abroad who voluntarily acquires a foreign nationality loses Chinese nationality automatically.

Between those two clauses sits the position a surrogacy-born child often occupies: a baby holding a passport from the country of birth, whom the consulate may treat as a Chinese national, and who is a minor with no capacity to naturalise voluntarily. The published text contains no clause by which such a person elects one nationality at eighteen. The exits the law offers are automatic loss under Article 9, which requires the person to be settled abroad and to take a foreign nationality by their own choice, or an application to renounce, whose grounds [the National Immigration Administration lists](https://www.nia.gov.cn/n1051057/n1051076/c1051258/content.html) as close relatives of foreigners, settlement abroad, or other legitimate reasons.

That is a gap in the published material rather than a settled answer, and it is a question for a lawyer who practises in that system. What can be said from the text is narrower and worth holding onto: a child whom China treats as its own national will be handled as a Chinese national by Chinese authorities, whatever passport sits in the child's file.

## Registering the child at home

For a family that will live in China, the practical end of the chain is household registration, the record that nearly every other admin in a person's life is looked up from. [One district sub-office's published service guide](https://gc.jiceng.zhengzhou.gov.cn/004001/3543190.jhtml) lists what it requires for a child born abroad to Chinese-national parents:

- The passport or travel document the child held on entry.
- The birth certificate issued by the competent or notary authority of the country of birth.
- One parent's resident identity card and the household registration book.
- The parents' marriage certificate.
- Chinese translations of any foreign-language document.
- A paternity report from a qualified domestic institution where parentage is not established on the record.

Two things about that list matter more than its length. It demands the document from the section above, which is why a consulate sits on your critical path rather than on your itinerary. And it demands a Chinese translation of everything foreign, which is a separate requirement, with a separate authority and a separate fee.

That guide is one sub-office's published list in one city. No nationally binding published rule for registering a child born through surrogacy was located in the material read for this page, so the ask is to get the requirement in writing from the office that will process it.

What a newspaper investigation describes is messier than the list. [Reporting published in February 2026](https://news.southcn.com/node_64549305f1/90f15d5d04.shtml) describes the mainland's own medical birth certificate as the practical gateway to registration, with hospitals declining to issue one where the woman who gave birth and the intended parents do not match the record, and with families reported to have cleared the obstacle through a DNA test with police verification, or by producing a formal notice that the certificate would not be issued. That is reporting rather than a published rule, and it is the reason to put this question to a lawyer in the registration system before putting it to an agency.

One more piece of context, from the service end rather than the family end. [Article 22 of the administrative measures on human assisted reproductive technology](https://xxgk.jl.gov.cn/zcbm/fgw_98077/zcfg/201904/t20190409_5779895.html) subjects institutions and personnel that implement surrogacy technology to a warning and a fine of up to 30,000 yuan, decided by the provincial health administration. Whatever weight a reader gives that, the practical consequence is visible in the list above: the paperwork a child needs at home has to come from outside the mainland's own health system.

## The flight out is not guaranteed

[Australia's government tells its own citizens something every family should read before it books a return date](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/planning-trip/advice-your-planned-activities/going-overseas-international-surrogacy). Its travel advisory warns that the authorities of the country of birth may not let the child or the parents leave, that some families waited months or years to resolve legal issues, that the Australian Government cannot get involved in visa or legal matters, and that a valid passport is required to enter Australia.

That is one government's advice to its own nationals, and it is not a statement about your country. Read the equivalent page for whichever passport your child will travel on, because it will say the same thing in that system's terms: the exit is decided by an authority you do not control, on a schedule you cannot buy.

## No treaty sits behind any of this

The reason the chain above is fragile is not that any one country is unreasonable. It is that no instrument makes one country's parentage decision travel to another. [The Hague Conference on Private International Law](https://www.hcch.net/en/projects/legislative-projects/parentage-surrogacy) ran a project on legal parentage resulting from international surrogacy arrangements through delegate meetings from 2016 to 2022, and at its 2026 session its governing body decided not to advance to a special commission to draft a possible convention at this stage. The file returns for review at the 2028 council meeting.

So every authority in the chain applies its own rules to documents it did not produce. An apostille removes one formal step from that and nothing more: it certifies who signed, and the receiving authority still decides what else it wants. A six-month clock on a court application in the receiving country is the same problem in time form.

## Questions to put to each system

Take these to a lawyer in each country involved and write each answer beside the name of the office or person who gave it. The [destination research checklist](/tools/destination-research-checklist) is built for exactly that.

1. At the time of the birth, will either Chinese-national parent hold permanent residence anywhere outside China, and does the answer change which document the consulate issues?
2. Which court or registry decides legal parentage in the place of birth, what does it require from you, and how long between the birth and the order?
3. Where in this chain is a genetic link required, and where is a gestational relationship enough?
4. What does the registration office in the country the child will live in require, in writing, including translations and any paternity report?
5. Who physically holds each document between the birth and the flight, and how do you get the originals back?

Question five is not administrative. During a cross border process the people who physically hold a birth certificate, a court order and a passport application are usually a lawyer, an agency or a courier, and a plan that has not named which is a plan whose child's papers are somewhere in transit.
